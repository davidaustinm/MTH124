    Handout Section 2.3: The Sine and Cosine Functions

    MTH 124
    🔗
    Have your unit circles out! Use them as a reference. We are going to turn each coordinate of the points on the unit circle into a new function.
    🔗

    Definition 74 Definition 2.3.4.

    Given a central angle in the unit circle with angle \(t\) radians and the terminal point \((a,b)\text{,}\) as shown in the graph, we define the sine of \(t\), denoted \(\sin(t)\text{,}\) by the rule
    \begin{equation*} \sin(t) = b. \end{equation*}
    That is, the \(y\)-coordinate on the unit circle IS the \(\sin(t)\text{!}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 75 Definition 2.3.8..

    Given a central angle in the unit circle that measures \(t\) radians and the terminal point \((a,b)\text{,}\) as shown in the graph, we define the cosine of \(t\), denoted \(\cos(t)\text{,}\) by the rule
    \begin{equation*} \cos(t) = a. \end{equation*}
    That is, the \(x\)-coordinate on the unit circle IS the \(\cos(t)\text{!}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We’ll start with the sine function. A table of values and a graph of the function \(\sin(t)\) are below.

    Activity 76 Activity 2.3.2. The cosine function.

    Let \(k=g(t)\) be the function that tracks the \(x\)-coordinate of a point traversing the unit circle counterclockwise from \((1,0)\text{.}\) That is, \(g(t) = \cos(t)\text{.}\) Use the information we know about the unit circle to respond to the following questions.
    🔗

    (a)

    What is the exact value of \(\cos(\frac{\pi}{6})\text{?}\) of \(\cos(\frac{5\pi}{6})\) ? \(\cos(-\frac{\pi}{3})\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    See the x-coordinate on the unit circle that corresponds to those angles.
    🔗
    \(\cos(\frac{\pi}{6}) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\) \(\cos(\frac{5\pi}{6}) = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\text{.}\) \(\cos(-\frac{\pi}{3}) = \frac12\) (same angle as \(\frac{5\pi}{3}\) or think of it as going "backwards" or clockwise by an angle of \(\frac{\pi}{3}\text{.}\))

    (d)

    What is the exact value of \(\cos(\frac{11\pi}{4})\text{?}\) of \(\cos(\frac{14\pi}{3})\) ?
    🔗
    Solution.
    *Going around the circle by \(2\pi\) doesn’t change the angle, it just changes the number of times around the circle. So an angle of \(\frac{11\pi}{4}\) is equivalent to an angle of
    \begin{equation*} \frac{11\pi}{4} - 2\pi = \frac{11\pi}{4}-\frac{2\pi*4}{4} = \frac{11\pi}{4}-\frac{8\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}. \end{equation*}
    So \(\cos(\frac{11\pi}{4}) = \cos(\frac{11\pi}{4}-2\pi) = \cos(\frac{3\pi}{4})=-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\text{.}\) Similarly, \(\frac{14\pi}{3}\) is more than two full laps around the circle, so that angle is equivalent to the angle
    \begin{equation*} \frac{14\pi}{3} - 2*(2\pi) = \frac{14\pi}{3} - \frac{4\pi * 3}{1*3} = \frac{14\pi}{3}-\frac{12\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3} \end{equation*}
    so \(\cos(\frac{14\pi}{3}) = \cos(\frac{14\pi}{3}-2*(2\pi)) = \cos(\frac{2\pi}{3}) = -\frac12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Give four different values of \(t\) for which \(\cos(t) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The easiest ones to see are \(t = \frac{5\pi}{6}, \frac{7\pi}{6}\text{,}\) but we can add or subtract any multiple of \(2\pi\) to those and be at the same point on the unit circle, so
    \begin{equation*} t = \ldots, \frac{5\pi}{6}, \frac{7\pi}{6}, \frac{17\pi}{6}, \frac{19\pi}{6}, \frac{29\pi}{6}, \frac{31\pi}{6}, \ldots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    How is the graph of \(\cos(t)\) different from the graph of \(\sin(t)\text{?}\) How are the graphs similar?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Lots of things you could say. The biggest difference (according to me) is the starting point - cosine starts at 1 not 0, that is \(\cos(0) = 1\) whereas \(\sin(0)=0\text{,}\) so cosine starts at the top of its range and goes down first whereas sine starts in the middle and goes up first.
    🔗
    They are exactly the same shape, just shifted by \(\frac\pi2\) to the right/left!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall that the sine and cosine functions are just tracking the \(x\) and \(y\)-coordinates as we move around the unit circle. See gvsu.edu/s/0xe and click on sine or cosine for an animation.
    🔗

    Properties of the sine and cosine functions.

    For both \(f(t) = \sin(t)\) and \(g(t) =\cos(t)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • the domain is all real numbers
      🔗
      🔗
    • the range is \([-1,1]\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • the midline is \(y=0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • the amplitude is \(a=1\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • the period is \(p=2\pi\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The Fundamental Trigonometric Identity.

    For any real number \(t\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2(t) + \cos^2(t) = 1. \end{equation*}

    Activity 77 Activity 2.3.3.

    Use the graph below that shows both \(\sin(t)\) and \(\cos(t)\) on the same plot to help answer the following questions.
    🔗

    (a)

    Give an example of the largest interval you can find on which \(\sin(t)\) is decreasing.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The most obvious from this graph is \(\left(\frac\pi2, \frac{3\pi}2\right)\text{,}\) but there are infinitely many if we shift (right/left) by a multiple of \(2\pi\text{.}\) (Same as \(\frac\pi2 \lt t \lt \frac{3\pi}2\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Give an example of the largest interval you can find on which \(\sin(t)\) is decreasing and concave down.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The \(\sin(t)\) is concave down (pessimistic) whenever it is above the midline \(y=0\text{.}\) It is also decreasing in that second half of that interval. So one example interval is \(\left(\frac\pi2, \pi\right)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Give an example of the largest interval you can find on which \(\cos(t)\) is increasing.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The most obvious from the graph is \((\pi,2\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Give an example of the largest interval you can find on which \(\cos(t)\) is increasing and concave up.
    🔗
    Solution.
    It is concave up when it is below the midline, and it is also increasing on the second half of that interval, so one example is \((\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Without doing any computation, on which interval is the average rate of change of \(g(t)=\cos(t)\) greater: \([\pi,\pi+0.01]\) or \([\frac{3\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}+0.01]\text{?}\) Why?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The slope around \([\pi,\pi+0.01]\) is about 0 (it’s flat at the top), but between \([\frac{3\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}+0.01]\) it has the steepest slope.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    In general, how would you characterize the locations on the sine and cosine graphs where the functions are increasing or decreasingly most rapidly?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Sine is increasing/decreasing most rapidly around \(t\approx 0, \pi, 2\pi, \ldots\) and cosine around \(t\approx \frac\pi2, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}2, \ldots\) that is near the midline for both!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    Thinking from the perspective of the unit circle, for which quadrants of the \(x\)-\(y\) plane is \(\cos(t)\) negative for an angle \(t\) that lies in that quadrant?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Cosine is the x-coordinate, which is negative in quadrants II and III.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 78 Activity 2.2.4 Going between.

    Note that almost all computing devices can evaluate \(\sin(t)\) and \(\cos(t)\) for different values of \(t\text{,}\) but we need to be careful to tell it whether we are in degrees or radians! In desmos, the option to choose is within the wrench at the top right of the page. Being aware of that, find the exact values whenever possible, otherwise round to at least 4 decimal places below.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find the \(x\)-coordinate of the point on the unit circle that lies in the third quadrant and whose \(y\)-coordinate is \(y=-\frac34\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(y=-\frac34\text{,}\) the Fundamental Trig Identity says \({\ds x^2 + (-\frac34)^2 = 1}\text{,}\) that is \({\ds x^2 + \frac9{16} = 1}\text{.}\) Rearranging: \({x^2 = 1-\frac9{16} = \frac{16}{16}-\frac{9}{16} = \frac{7}{16}}\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} x = \pm \frac{\sqrt{7}}{4} \end{equation*}
    but we are in quadrant III and so the x-coordinate must be negative. Thus \({x = -\frac{\sqrt7}{4}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the \(y\)-coordinate of the point on the unit circle generated by a central angle opening counterclockwise with one side on the positive \(x\)-axis that measures \(t=2\) radians.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\frac\pi2\approx 1.57\) and \(\pi \approx 3.14\) then \(2\) radians is in between \(\frac\pi2\) and \(\pi\text{,}\) that is we are in Quadrant II, where the y-coordinate is positive. So \(y=\sin(2)\) is the exact answer, and it turns into approximately \(0.9093\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the \(x\)-coordinate of the point on the unit circle generated by a central angle with one side on the positive \(x\)-axis that measures \(t=-3.05\) radians. (With the negative radian measure, we view the angle as opening clockwise from its initial side on the positive \(x\)-axis.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We end up in Quadrant III (we go "backwards" or clockwise by \(3.05\) radians which is just short of \(\pi\) radians). \(x = \cos(-3.05) \approx -0.996\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Find the value of \(\cos(t)\) where \(t\) is an angle in Quadrant II that satisfies \(\sin(t)=\frac12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We are looking for the point in Quadrant II that corresponds to \(\sin(t)=\frac12\text{,}\) or the y-coordinate is \(\frac12\text{.}\) This happens at \(t=\frac{5\pi}{6}\) (which I don’t need to know, because the x-y coordinates come in pairs I know...) so the x-coordinate is \(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) or in other words \({\cos(t) = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Find the value of \(\sin(t)\) where \(t\) is an angle in Quadrant III for which \(\cos(t) = -0.7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In Quadrant III, both \(\sin(t)\) and \(\cos(t)\) are negative. Fundamental Trig Identity: \(\sin^2(t)+\cos^2(t) = 1\) or since \(\cos(t)=-0.7\) then
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2(t)+(-0.7)^2 = 1 \end{equation*}
    or \(\sin^2(t) = 0.51\) and since \({\sin^2(t) = (\sin(t))^2}\text{,}\) then \({(\sin(t))^2 = 0.51}\) or \(\sin(t) =\pm 0.714\text{.}\) Since we are in Quadrant III, then \(\sin(t) = -0.714\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Find the average rate of change of \(f(t) = \sin(t)\) on the intervals \([0.1,0.2]\) and \([0.8,0.9]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} AV_{[0.1,0.2]} = \frac{\sin(0.2) - \sin(0.1)}{0.2-0.1}\approx 0.988 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} AV_{[0.8,0.9]} = \frac{\sin(0.9) - \sin(0.8)}{0.9-0.8}\approx 0.6597 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    Find the average rate of change of \(g(t) = \cos(t)\) on the intervals \([0.1,0.2]\) and \([0.8,0.9]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} AV_{[0.1,0.2]} = \frac{\cos(0.2) - \cos(0.1)}{0.2-0.1}\approx -0.149 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} AV_{[0.8,0.9]} = \frac{\cos(0.9) - \cos(0.8)}{0.9-0.8}\approx -0.751 \end{equation*}
