    Subsection First Things First

    Things to keep in mind throughout this course.
    1. The whole point of education is to change your mind: to make your mind better than it used to be. Be open to changing what, how, and why you think about mathematics, and work hard to train your mind to get better at thinking about mathematics and thinking generally.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. I want you to be successful. I define “successful” in MTH 201 to include: changing your mind by learning a lot (see #1), developing a deep understanding of differential calculus that you can demonstrate to others, becoming able to apply ideas in calculus to solve interesting problems, working well with your classmates, and improving your ability to communicate about complex ideas with clarity and precision. I do not think of success in terms of letter grades.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Strive to embrace struggle and have a growth mindset. Nothing that is worth doing comes easily, and struggle and even failure are a valuable and necessary part of growth (think about playing the piano, hitting a golf ball, being a good friend … all are difficult and require mistakes and failure in order to get better). Be prepared to be sometimes frustrated with the course. Work to have a growth mindset: the whole reason you are taking the class is to learn new things and make sense of them. Persistence and hard work count for a lot -- in this class and in life.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Learn from and with others. In almost any career, you’ll work as part of a team. Doing so will require you to be a contributor, be a communicator, and be a supporter. As most people don’t get to choose their co-workers, we also need to develop our ability to work with a wide range of people; you’ll be even better as a co-worker if you can appreciate others and the unique contributions they bring. Every day in class, you’ll have the opportunity to work with peers to learn with and learn from them. Indeed, we want to be a community for one another: be positive, be kind, and be caring. I will.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Embrace learning actively. The only person who can learn and develop understanding in your own mind is you. While I cannot learn for you, I can and will offer an environment that supports your learning with structured work inside and outside of class designed to help you make more sense of calculus. Through all of it, I will be here to support you with perspective and guidance.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Take good care of yourself and others. We are living in an ongoing pandemic and we are tired of it. It’s more important than ever that you sleep well, exercise, practice good hygiene, and seek help when you need it. If you are struggling with physical or mental health, you can talk to me about how you are doing without judgment and I will help you find support from professional resources.
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Equity. We live in a world where not all groups of people are treated equitably. Please know that I am fully committed to all forms of equity and that in all aspects of this course I will not tolerate language, ideas, or behavior that are hostile to marginalized or oppressed groups, such as racism, sexism, homophobia, transphobia, and xenophobia. In new situations, people are sometimes uncertain about what language and ideas could be hostile or offensive. If you feel unsure, I am more than willing to discuss these issues with you privately. I will always treat these conversations confidentially, with respect, and without judgment.
      🔗
      🔗

    AI Policy.

    This is complicated, and we’ll talk about it in class, but please don’t use generative AI to complete any assignments or to check your work in this course. You may think:
    • “I will be using AI in my career.” With the current state of AI, you won’t be using it to learn new ways of thinking, like we’re trying to do in our course. AI will help you develop an understanding of mathematics.
      🔗
      🔗
    • “I only use it to check my work when I don’t feel totally confident.” I am part of a math education research study that investigates how students learn math from textbooks. Interviews with students clearly show how using AI causes students to become less confident over time since they come to trust what comes from AI more than they trust their own work. Learning will be challenging and facing challenges requires you to have the confidence that you can do it. Please don’t surrender the trust that you have in your own abilities.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Here is one research study, among many others: Your Brain on ChatGPT. From the abstract:
    Over four months, LLM users consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels. These results raise concerns about the long-term educational implications of LLM reliance and underscore the need for deeper inquiry into AI’s role in learning.

    Subsection About this course

    What is PreCalculus?

    Precalculus is meant to prepare GVSU students for calculus. The focus is to understand how math can be used to model and help us better understand the world around us. Math is less about the right answer, and much more about reasoning, critical thinking, problem solving, and making connections. Important goals of this class are also to be able to work creatively and independently, and to collaborate and communicate well with others.
    🔗
    🔗

    Course Description from the Catalog.

    Study of preparatory material for calculus using symbolic algebra and trigonometry for solving equations, representing functions, and modeling, plus appropriate technology. Core topics: concept of function, average rate of change of a function, inverse and composite functions, trigonometric functions, exponential and logarithmic functions, and right triangle trigonometry.
    🔗
    🔗

    Course Objectives.

    After successful completion of this course, you will be able to…
    1. Demonstrate broad algebraic and trigonometric skills for manipulations expressions, solving equations, and understanding and representing functions, along with graphical and numerical perspectives that support analytical work.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Identify the average rate of change of a function and be able to compute and interpret it, with units, in a wide range of settings.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Demonstrate communication and problem-solving skills that enable you to meaningfully investigate, discuss, and solve authentic problems using proper language, syntax, and notation, both orally and in writing.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Demonstrate understanding of the concept of a function, and use functions to build models of real-world quantitative phenomena.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Use a variety of computing technology as tools for making sense of mathematical concepts, creating and exploring models, and developing communications skills.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Workload.

    This is a five-credit class, which carries an expectation of roughly 10 hours of work each week outside of class. This course also aims to prepare you for calculus, and will help you hone skills such as critical thinking, problem solving, communication of complex ideas, or working with others. It is more important than ever to be able to solve non-routine problems and be confident in learning new ideas. If you work to make sense of ideas, you will be able to think creatively in new ways.

    Questions are always encouraged, never judged, and always answered to the best of abilities.

    Subsection Assessments and Grades

    The overall grades.

    Assigning grades is the probably the most frustrating aspect of teaching. Each of you is a unique learner who brings a different perspective and background into our course. It’s therefore important to build some flexibility into any grading scheme. Given below is the percentage required to obtain a particular grade. However, I reserve the right to raise your grade if I see that you are working diligently and that you grow intellectually during the semester.
    🔗
    A A- B+ B B- C+ C C- D+ D F
    93% 90% 87% 83% 80% 77% 73% 70% 67% 63% \(\lt\) 63%

    Determination of Grades.

    Your grade in this class will be determined in the following way:
    Daily Prep (DP) - 5%
    🔗
    There will be an assignment due before class on Tuesdays and Thursdays that is meant to get you thinking about the content. They will be accessible in Desmos, and the questions will be graded based on effort and participation, not correctness.
    🔗
    Attendance (A) - 5%
    🔗
    I will take attendance in this class and it will count towards your grade. You can make up up to four missed classes by following the instructions above in the section "Attendance and making up attendance."
    🔗
    Online Homework or WeBWorK (WW) - 10%
    🔗
    WeBWork is an online homework platform. There will be a WeBWork set each week consisting of 10 exercises per set, relating to the content of the course. Each exercise is worth 1 point, and so each set will be worth 10 points. For each problem, you will have 8 attempts to answer; before you reach your 8th try, come and talk to me! Your lowest WeBWork score will be dropped.
    🔗
    Written Homework (HW) - 24%
    🔗
    Homework problems are more extended and involved multi-part problems to solve that involve a formal writeup. You are expected to give solutions, not just answers. Each problem is marked on a scale of 10 points, and there will be approximately 1 per week. You can resubmit up to 2 problems per week on problems that were submitted on time (or with a deadline extension) and completed with effort.
    🔗
    Please see the "Written work expectations" section below.
    🔗
    Targets (T) - 36%
    🔗
    Each week Monday in class you’ll complete a weekly checkpoint to assess your understanding of up to 4 CORE learning targets in the course. The 12 learning targets are outlined below in the next section. Each target receives “Excellent,” “Satisfactory,” or “Not Yet.”
    🔗
    You’ll have two attempts at each of the 12 CORE learning targets in class on Mondays over two consecutive checkpoints. If you earn “Excellent” target 1 on the one checkpoint, you do not need to answer that target’s question on the next checkpoint, but if you receive a “Satisfactory” or “Not Yet”, you have one more checkpoint to earn an “Excellent” mark. If you need a third attempt on a target, you need to come to see me within two weeks of getting back the feedback.
    🔗
    • “E” = “Excellent”: you get full credit for this target, 1 point out of 1 point, so each “E” target is worth 3% of your total grade.
      🔗
      🔗
    • “S” = “Satisfactory”: you are very close to an “Excellent”, but there is something that still can be improved, 0.75 points out of 1 point.
      🔗
      🔗
    • “NY” = “Not Yet”: you don’t quite have it yet, but have opportunities to try again, 0 points out of 1 point.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Final Exam - 20%
    🔗
    The final exam will consist of questions that address big-picture ideas in the course. More details to come.

    CORE Learning Targets.

    The following are the 12 Core Learning Targets for this semester. The successful demonstration of Excellence (E) on one of these targets is worth 3% of your semester grade, therefore Excellence on all the targets is worth 36% of your grade. You will have up to 2 attempts at each of these targets during your weekly checkpoints, where each week up to 4 targets may appear. You may have an additional attempt during office hours, or during an appointment with Dr. Bailey. Once you have demonstrated Excellence for a specific target on a weekly checkpoint, you will no longer need to answer the question related to that target on the other checkpoints.
    1. I can use function notation, find and simplify the output of a function given an input, and find an input that produces a given output; I can do this for functions given by a formula, a table, and a graph.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. I can find the average rate of change in a function on a given interval and state the units and interpret the meaning of the average rate of change in applied contexts.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. I can find an equation for any linear function in point-slope and slope-intercept form, given appropriate data or information.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. I can find and simplify values of the composition of two functions.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. I can determine whether a function has an inverse and find values of the inverse given input-output pairs of the original.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. I can generate an accurate, labeled graph of a circular function given appropriate information about the circle being traversed, and describe key features of the function.
      🔗
      🔗
    7. I can find the amplitude, period, and midline of a transformed version of the basic sine or cosine function; and I can find the formulas for transformed versions of the basic sine or cosine function that have certain described properties.
      🔗
      🔗
    8. Given two points or an appropriate collection of features, I can find a formula for an exponential function that has those characteristics.
      🔗
      🔗
    9. I can identify the intervals where a function is increasing, decreasing, concave down, or concave up, and I can provide examples of familiar functions that have given increasing / decreasing and concave down / concave up behaviors.
      🔗
      🔗
    10. I can find exact values for exponential models and exact values of solutions to equations involving exponential and logarithmic expressions in contextual settings.
      🔗
      🔗
    11. Given a right triangle with partial information about its sides and angles, I can determine the missing information for the remaining sides and angles, even if the quantities involve an unknown variable.
      🔗
      🔗
    12. I can identify the degree, coefficients, constant and linear terms, and leading coefficient of a polynomial, and identify zeroes, the multiplicity of zeroes, and turning points of a polynomial given its graph.
      🔗
      🔗

    Written work expectations.

    One important part of mathematics is its emphasis on the clear and careful presentation of reasoning. This includes clearly stating the problem, making important observations in complete sentences, writing additional thoughts to clarify symbolic expressions, and showing a clear overall progression from problem to solution. The quality of your communication will count in all of your graded work. You will generally be marked on the methods used to solve a problem rather than just the final answer; answers alone will receive little credit. Therefore, it is very important that you include all of your work when writing a solution. The clarity with which you express your thinking will be an important part of your grade as well.
    🔗
    Please take advantage of the opportunity to resubmit homework assignments after incorporating my feedback. I want you to be motivated from within, not by a somewhat arbitrary grade, and I want to allow you the opportunity to make mistakes and learn from them. It is my belief that you will learn more if you are internally motivated and able to resubmit your work.
    🔗
    "The goal of training a mathematician is not achieved by producing correct homework." ~Terry Tao. Terry Tao is a very famous mathematician. You can read his full essay for free, Mathematics in the Age of AI.
    🔗
    🔗

    Feedback or concerns.

    Any thoughts, concerns, ideas are welcome, including those concerning either your performance or mine. Please let me know if there is something that is working well for you or if I can be doing better.

    Academic Integrity Policies.

    Students are expected to be familiar with and comply with GVSU’s academic misconduct policies and procedures (sections 5.1 and 6.1) in the Student Code: The Anchor of Student Rights and Responsibilities. I generally have a zero-tolerance policy for academic misconduct, and all suspected violations will result in a referral to the Student Integrity Team in the Dean of Students Office. If you have any doubts or questions about what constitutes academic misconduct, please do not hesitate to contact me.
    🔗
    Plagiarism: Any ideas or material taken from another source for either written or oral presentation must be fully acknowledged. Offering the work of someone else as one’s own is plagiarism. The language or ideas taken from another may range from isolated formulas, sentences, or paragraphs to entire articles copied from books, periodicals, speeches, or the writing of other students. The offering of materials assembled or collected by others in the form of projects or collections without acknowledgment also is considered plagiarism. Any student who fails to give credit in written or oral work for the ideas or materials that have been taken from another is guilty of plagiarism.
