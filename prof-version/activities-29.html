    Handout Section 3.6: Modeling Temperature and Population
    

    MTH 124
    🔗

    Recall Properties and Newton’s Law of Cooling.

    • Relation: \({e^a = b \iff \ln(b) = a}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Inverses: \({\ln(e^x) = x, \ \ \ e^{\ln(x)}=x}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Multi. inside: \({\ln(a\cdot b) = \ln(a)+\ln(b)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Division inside: \({\ln(\frac{a}{b})=\ln(a)-\ln(b)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Powers come out: \({\ln(a^t)=t\ln(a)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Long term: \({e^{kt} \rightarrow \infty, \ \ e^{-kt}\rightarrow 0}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Temp of a warming/cooling object (\(k\gt 0\)):
    \begin{equation*} F(t) = ae^{-kt}+c \end{equation*}
    1. \(c\) is surrounding temp/long-term behavior
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(a\) is NOT initial temp! But \(F(0)=a+c\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(k\) is found from one other temp at \(t\ne 0\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 122.

    You’re cooking a turkey. It is initially room temperature which is \(68^\circ\) F. You cook it in a \(350^{\circ}\) F oven. After 50 minutes the turkey is \(100^\circ\) F. What is the temperature after 80 minutes?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We know the following: our model has the form \(F(t)=ae^{-kt}+c\) (Newton’s Law of Cooling).
    🔗
    The initial temperature is \(68^\circ\) so \(F(0)=68\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The long-term temperature is the temp of the oven which is \(350^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    When \(t=50\text{,}\) \(F(50) = 100^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1: Long term temperature is long term behavior which is \(c\text{,}\) so \(c=350\text{.}\) The model then becomes \({F(t)=ae^{-kt}+350}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Initial temp is not \(a\text{,}\) but \(F(0)=68\) and plugging \(0\) into the model it is also \(F(0)=a+350\text{,}\) so \(68 = a+350\text{.}\) Rearranging, we get \(a=68-350=-282\text{.}\) Thus the model becomes \({F(t)=-282e^{-kt}+350}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: The last point. We know \(F(50)=100\text{,}\) so plugging in
    \begin{align*} 100 \amp = -282e^{-50k}+350\\ -250 \amp = -282e^{-50k}\\ \frac{250}{282} \amp = e^{-50k}\\ \ln(\frac{250}{282}) \amp = {-50k}\\ k \amp = \dfrac{\ln(250/282)}{-50} \end{align*}
    So the entire model becomes
    \begin{equation*} F(t) = -282e^{-\frac{\ln(250/282)}{-50} t} + 350. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now the final piece is that we want the temp after 80 minutes, we want \(F(80)\text{,}\) so we plug in:
    \begin{equation*} F(80) = -282e^{-\frac{\ln(250/282)}{-50} (80)} + 350. \end{equation*}
    In more sensible, approximate terms, \(F(80)\approx 117.4\text{,}\) so the turkey is about \(117.4^\circ\) after 80 minutes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 123. Activity 3.6.2.

    A can of soda is initially at room temperature, \(72.3^{\circ}\) Fahrenheit, and at time \(t=0\) is placed in a refrigerator set at \(37.7^{\circ}\text{.}\) In addition, we know that after \(30\) minutes, the soda’s temperature has dropped to \(59.5^{\circ}\text{.}\) Let \(F(t)\) represent the temperature of the soda in degrees Fahrenheit at time \(t\) in minutes.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use algebraic reasoning and your understanding of the physical situation to determine the exact values of \(a\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) and \(k\) in the model \(F(t)=ae^{-kt}+c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (Find \(c\) then \(a\) then \(k\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Solution.
    Step 1: The long-term temperature is the temperature of the refrigerator which is also \(c\) (the horizontal asymptote/vertical shift), so \(c=37.7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Initial value. Since \(F(0)=a+37.7=72.3\text{,}\) we get \(a=72.3-37.7=34.6\text{.}\) So \(F(t)=34.6e^{-kt}+37.7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: Another point. We know \(F(30)=59.5\text{,}\) so we plug in and are left with \(k\) as the only variable, which we can solve for:
    \begin{align*} 59.5 \amp = 34.6e^{-30k}+37.7\\ 21.8 \amp = 34.6e^{-30k}\\ \dfrac{21.8}{34.6} \amp = e^{-30k}\\ \ln(\dfrac{21.8}{34.6}) \amp = {-30k}\\ k \amp = \dfrac{\ln(21.8/34.6)}{-30} \end{align*}
    So we can plug that into our model, and we know an equation for \(F(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Determine the exact time the object’s temperature is \(42.4^{\circ}\text{.}\) Clearly show your work. Hint: it may help to leave \(k\) as \(k\) until you get \(t=...\text{,}\) then plug in \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We’re going to leave \(k\) as \(k\) for now, and then plug it in at the very last step, because that seems a lot easier. So we want to find the time \(t\) when \(F(t)=42.4\) and note that we are solving for \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} 42.4 \amp = 34.6e^{-kt}+37.7 \\ 4.7 \amp = 34.6e^{-kt} \\ \dfrac{4.7}{34.6} \amp =e^{-kt}\\ \ln(\dfrac{4.7}{34.6}) \amp ={-kt}\\ -kt \amp = \ln\left(\dfrac{4.7}{34.6}\right)\\ t \amp = \dfrac{\ln(4.7/34.6)}{-k} \end{align*}
    That seems nicer. Now we can plug in \(k=\dfrac{\ln(21.8/34.6)}{-30}\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} t = \dfrac{\ln(4.7/34.6)}{-(\dfrac{\ln(21.8/34.6)}{-30})} \end{gather*}
    Still gross, but kind of great at the same time. It turns out this is approximately \(t\approx 129.6\) minutes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    In Desmos, enter the values you found for \(a\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) and \(k\) in order to define the function \({F(t)=ae^{-kt}+c}\text{.}\) Then, use Desmos to find the average rate of change of \(F\) on the interval \([25,30]\text{.}\) What is the meaning (with units) of this value?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The average rate of change on \([25,30]\) is
    \begin{equation*} AV_{[25,30]} = \dfrac{F(30)-F(25)}{30-25} \approx \dfrac{59.5-61.24}{5} \approx -0.349. \end{equation*}
    The units are \(^\circ F/min\text{.}\) Between \(t=25\) and \(t=30\) minutes, the soda’s temperature drops on average about \(0.349^\circ\) F each minute.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 124.

    Reality check: Populations don’t usually grow forever with no limits, resources limit growth. Calculus can help us develop a more realistic model called the Logistic Model.
    🔗
    🔗

    Logistic Model.

    of population growth with positive constants \(A, M, k\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = \frac{A}{1+Me^{-kt}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since, \(e^{-kt}\rightarrow 0\text{,}\) then \(\frac{A}{1+Me^{-kt}}\rightarrow \frac{A}{1+0} \rightarrow A\text{!}\) Thus \(P(t)\rightarrow A\) in the long-term.
    🔗
    ** \(A\) is the carrying capacity of the population, i.e. the long-term behavior/max capacity.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 125. Activity 3.6.3. Logistic Model.

    In Desmos, define \(P(t)=\frac{A}{1+Me^{-kt}}\) and accept sliders for \(A\text{,}\) \(M\text{,}\) and \(k\text{.}\) Set the slider ranges for these parameters as follows: \(0.01 \le A \le 10\text{;}\) \(0.01 \le M \le 10\text{;}\) \(0.01\le k \le 5\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Sketch a typical graph of \(P(t)\) on the axes provided and write several sentences to explain the effects of \(A\text{,}\) \(M\text{,}\) and \(k\) on the graph of \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    See Desmos to see what it looks like, but it should look like and S-like curve. It is always increasing, and appears to level off over time.
    🔗
    • \(A\text{:}\) the carrying capacity, this acts as a vertical stretch and a maximum output, so this also is the long-term behavior.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(M\text{:}\) appears to affect the starting value, and acts similar to a horizontal shift (left/right). Not exactly, but it does affect the horizontal direction only.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(k\text{:}\) this appears to act like a horizontal stretch, and makes the S-curve either steeper or shallower depending on the value.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    On a typical logistic graph, where does it appear that the population is growing most rapidly? How is this value connected to the carrying capacity, \(A\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The population appears grows most fastest (the steepest part of the graph), in the middle, which looks to be halfway between 0 and the carrying capacity \(A\text{,}\) so around \(y=A/2\text{.}\) Notice also this middle point appears to be where the graph changes from concave up to concave down.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use your Desmos worksheet to find a logistic function \(P\) that has the following properties: \(P(0)=2\text{,}\) \(P(2)=4\text{,}\) and \(P(t)\) approaches \(9\) as \(t\) increases without bound. What are the approximate values of \(A\text{,}\) \(M\text{,}\) and \(k\) that make the function \(P\) fit these criteria?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Somewhere around \(P(t)= \frac{9}{1+\frac72 e^{-0.516t}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 126. Activity 3.6.4.

    Suppose that a population of animals (measured in thousands) that lives on an island is known to grow according to the logistic model, where \(t\) is measured in years. We know the following information: \(P(0) = 2.45\text{,}\) \(P(3)=4.52\text{,}\) and given the resources availabe the island cannot support more than \(11.7\) thousand animals.
    🔗

    (a)

    Determine the values of \(A\text{,}\) \(M\text{,}\) and \(k\) in the logist model below. Try to find exact. (For approximate, if you round, include 4 decimal places).
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = \frac{A}{1+Me^{-kt}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The order is actually very similar to solving Newton’s Law of Cooling Model. Since \(A\) is the long-term behavior or eventual outcome, this is the easiest to find and ideally the first step. So \(A = 11.7\text{,}\) and \({P(t)= \frac{11.7}{1+Me^{-kt}}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Zeros are lovely, so just like in earlier problems we want to use the initial condition (which is not equal to \(M\text{!}\)). But \(P(0)=2.45\) so plugging in we have:
    \begin{align*} 2.45 \amp = \dfrac{11.7}{1+Me^0}\\ 2.45 \amp = \dfrac{11.7}{1+M} \end{align*}
    So getting the \(M\) out of the denominator by multiplying both sides by \((1+M)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} 2.45(1+M) \amp = 11.7\\ 2.45+2.45M \amp = 11.7\\ 2.45M \amp = 9.25\\ M \amp = \dfrac{9.25}{2.45} \end{align*}
    so the model becomes \({P(t)= \frac{11.7}{1+\frac{9.25}{2.45}e^{-kt}}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now we only have one thing left to find, \(k\text{,}\) so we go to the last piece of info which is the hardest (no zeros), and we know that \(P(3)=4.52\text{.}\) So plugging in (and sticking to the theme of solving exactly):
    \begin{equation*} 4.52 = \dfrac{11.7}{1+\frac{9.25}{2.45}e^{-3k}} \end{equation*}
    but to get the gross stuff out of the denominator we can multiply both sides by the entire denom:
    \begin{align*} 4.52 ({1+\frac{9.25}{2.45}e^{-3k}}) \amp = 11.7 \\ 1+\frac{9.25}{2.45}e^{-3k} \amp = \frac{11.7}{4.52} \\ \frac{9.25}{2.45}e^{-3k} \amp = \frac{11.7}{4.52} - 1 \\ e^{-3k} \amp = \frac{2.45}{9.25}(\frac{11.7}{4.52} - 1) \\ {-3k} \amp = \ln\left(\frac{2.45}{9.25}(\frac{11.7}{4.52} - 1)\right) \\ {k} \amp = \frac{-1}{3}\ln\left(\frac{2.45}{9.25}(\frac{11.7}{4.52} - 1)\right) \end{align*}
    Eek. This should be \(k\approx 0.2886\text{.}\) Unless I have a typo, which is likely.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the approximate time value when the population will be \(10\) (thousand). Show your work. Hint: You may find it easier to not plug in \(k\) and \(M\) until you get \(t=...\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We will leave \(k,M\) and deal with them later! That seems nicer.
    🔗
    So we need to solve for time \(t\) when \(P(t)=10\text{,}\) plugging in and remembering that we are trying to get \(t=...\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} 10 \amp = \dfrac{11.7}{1+Me^{-kt}}\\ 10(1+Me^{-kt}) \amp = 11.7\\ 1+Me^{-kt} \amp = 1.17\\ Me^{-kt} \amp = 0.17\\ e^{-kt} \amp = \dfrac{0.17}{M}\\ {-kt} \amp = \ln(\dfrac{0.17}{M})\\ t \amp = -\frac{1}{k}\ln\left(\dfrac{0.17}{M}\right) \end{align*}
    Now we have \(t\text{,}\) let’s plug in \(M, k\text{,}\) and let’s just approximate here since it doesn’t ask for exact. With the help of a calculator: \({t \approx 10.743}\) years.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Plot your model from (a) and check that its values match the desired characteristics. Then, use Desmos to compute the average rate of change of \(P\) on the intervals \([0,2]\text{,}\) \([2,4]\text{,}\) \([4,6]\text{,}\) and \([6,8]\text{.}\) What is the meaning (with units) of the values you’ve found? How is the population growing on these intervals?
    🔗
    Solution.
    See Desmos for a plot. We find
    • \(\displaystyle AV_{[0,2]}=\frac{P(2)-P(0)}{2-0}\approx0.65\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle AV_{[2,4]}\approx 0.796\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle AV_{[4,6]}\approx 0.836\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle AV_{[6,8]}\approx 0.747\)
      🔗
      🔗
    All of these are in thousands with units of animals per year, so the first one says that on average between years 0 and 2 the population was growing by about 650 animals each year.
    🔗
    The population always growing since the average rate of change is always positive, but it seems to grow faster and then slow down at some point.
