    Handout Section 2.2: The Unit Circle
    

    MTH 124
    🔗
    The unit circle is a circle of radius \(1\) that is centered at the origin, \((0,0)\text{.}\) A point on the circle has some coordinates \((x,y)\text{,}\) and we can form a right triangle with the \(x\)-axis where the horizontal leg has length \(|x|\text{,}\) the vertical leg has length \(|y|\text{,}\) and the hypotenuse (the radius of the circle) is length 1. Pythagorean Theorem then tells us that
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + y^2 = 1! \end{equation*}
    This means we can determine some coordinates “nicely.”
    🔗

    Definition 68 Definition 2.2.5.

    An angle whose vertex is at the center of a circle measures 1 radian provided the arc the angle intercepts on the circle equals the radius of the circle.
    🔗
    Which just means: the distance along the edge of the circle is the as the angle between the \(x-\) axis and the point on the circle! (This is why we use radians...)
    🔗
    🔗

    Converting between radians and degrees.

    \begin{equation*} 1 \text{ radian }= \frac{180}{\pi}^{\circ}, \quad \quad \quad \quad 1^{\circ} = \frac{\pi}{180} \text{ radians} \end{equation*}
    Equivalent to
    \begin{equation*} \pi \text{ radians } = 180^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 69.

    🔗

    Activity 71 Activity 2.2.3 Special points on the unit circle.

    In what follows, we work to understand key relationships in \(45^{\circ} - 45^{\circ} - 90^{\circ}\) and \(30^{\circ} - 60^{\circ} - 90^{\circ}\) right triangles. (This will help us find exact coordinates on the unit circle.)
    🔗

    (a)

    For the \(45^{\circ} - 45^{\circ} - 90^{\circ}\) triangle with legs of length \(x\) and \(y\) and hypotenuse of length 1, because the triangle is an isosceles triangle, what is the relationship between \(x\) and \(y\text{?}\) Use this fact to simplify the Pythagorean Theorem \(x^2+y^2 = 1\)- what are the exact values of \(x\) and \(y\text{?}\) (Exact means leave \(\pi\) ’s or square roots or fractions in your answer so you do not round!)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(x=y\) on isosceles triangles, so Pythagorean Theorem says \({x^2 + x^2 = 1}\text{,}\) or \({2x^2 = 1}\text{.}\) This means \({x^2 = \frac12}\) and \({x = \sqrt{\frac12} = \frac{\sqrt{1}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}\text{.}\) (Since \(x\) is a length we don’t need to include \(x=\pm\sqrt{\frac12}\)). Then \({x = y = \frac{\sqrt 2}{2}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now consider the \(30^{\circ} - 60^{\circ} - 90^{\circ}\) triangle with hypotenuse of length 1 and the longer leg (of length \(x\) ) lying along the positive \(x\)-axis. Reflect this triangle across the -axis, what special triangle is formed (look at the angles)? Can we use this to find the exact length of \(y\text{?}\) Once we have \(y\text{,}\) go back and find the exact value of \(x\) using \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With the reflection we have an equilateral triangle with all sides equal to 1! Since \(y\) is half of the length of this doubled triangle then \({y=\frac12}\text{.}\) Then Pythagorean Theorem: \({x^2+(\frac12)^2 = 1}\text{.}\) That is \({x^2 + \frac14 = 1}\) or \({x^2 = \frac34}\) or \({x = \frac{\sqrt 3}{\sqrt 4} = \frac{\sqrt 3}{4}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Note we can take the same \(30^{\circ} - 60^{\circ} - 90^{\circ}\) and flip it so \(y\) corresponds to the smaller, horizontal leg. What are the \(x\) and \(y\) values in this case? (Hint, by looking at (b) you can save yourself a lot of work!)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Switch the \(x, y\text{!}\) That is \({x = \frac12, \ y=\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    We know from the conversion factor from degrees to radians that an angle of \(30^{\circ}\) corresponds to an angle measuring \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) radians, \(45^{\circ}\) corresponds to \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) radians, and \(60^{\circ}\) corresponds to \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) radians. Use parts (a) - (c) to label the coordinate points \((x,y)\) with the exact values of \(x\) and \(y\) on the three triangles below.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The first picture corresponds the part b and so knowing we have the coordinate with the values of \((x,y)\) with the \(x,y\) for that triangle, then the point is at the coordinate \((\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}, \frac12)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The second picture corresponds the part a and so knowing we have the coordinate with the values of \((x,y)\) with the \(x,y\) for that triangle, then the point is at the coordinate \((\frac{\sqrt 2}{2}, \frac{\sqrt 2}2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The third picture corresponds the part c and so knowing we have the coordinate with the values of \((x,y)\) with the \(x,y\) for that triangle, then the point is at the coordinate \((\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt 3}2)\text{.}\)

    Connecting arc length and angles in non-unit circles.

    In a circle of radius \(r\text{,}\) a central angle measuring \(\theta\) radians intercepts an arc of the circle, and the arc has length \(s\) where
    \begin{equation*} s=r\theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 73 Activity 2.2.4 Non-unit circles.

    In a circle of radius \(r\text{,}\) each point of the unit circle is a distance \(r\) further away from the origin. So \((1,0)\) on the unit circle would correspond to \((r,0)\text{,}\) and so on. That is, each terminal point on the unit circle corresponds to the same point, except each coordinate is multiplied by the new radius \(r\text{.}\) Find the following values or points on the non-unit circles below exactly.
    🔗

    (a)

    In a circle of radius \(11\text{,}\) find the arc length intercepted by a central angle of \(\frac{5\pi}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(r=11\text{,}\) \(\theta=\frac{5\pi}{3}\text{,}\) \(s = ?\text{.}\) So \({s = r\theta = 11 (\frac{5\pi}3) = \frac{55\pi}{3}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    In a circle of radius \(3\text{,}\) find the central angle measure that intercepts an arc of length \(\frac{\pi}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(r = 3\text{,}\) \(s =\frac{\pi}{4}\text{,}\) \(\theta = ?\text{.}\) So \({s = r\theta \implies \frac{\pi}{4} = 3\theta \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{4}(\frac13) \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{12}}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the radius of the circle in which an angle of \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\) intercepts an arc of length \(\frac{\pi}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(r = ?\text{,}\)\(\theta = \frac{7\pi}{6}\text{,}\) \(s = \frac{\pi}{4}\text{.}\) So \({s = r\theta \implies \frac\pi4 = r (\frac{7\pi}{6}) \implies r = \frac37}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Find the exact coordinates of the point on the circle of radius \(5\) that lies \(\frac{25\pi}{6}\) units counterclockwise along the circle from \((5,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(r = 5\text{,}\) \(s = \frac{25\pi}{6}\text{,}\) \(\theta = ?\text{.}\) So \({s = r\theta \implies \frac{25\pi}{6} = 5\theta \implies \theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}}\text{.}\) So on the unit circle the coordinates corresponding to that angle are \({(-\frac{\sqrt3}2,\frac12)}\text{.}\) But we are on a circle of radius 5, so the coordinate is 5 times bigger, i.e. \({(-5\frac{\sqrt3}2,\frac52)}\text{.}\)
